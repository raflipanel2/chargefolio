Watt-hours vs mAh: read battery capacity correctly

A label can show a large mAh number without telling you how much energy a device can deliver.

ChargeFolio Editorial DeskReviewed 11 Nov 2026409 core words

Two labels, two different quantities

Milliamp-hours (mAh) describe electric charge. Watt-hours (Wh) describe energy. Charge without voltage is not a complete energy comparison, because a 5,000 mAh pack at 3.7 volts stores a different amount of energy from 5,000 mAh at 7.4 volts. Start by locating the rated voltage and the rated capacity on the actual product label or manufacturer documentation.

When both numbers refer to the same battery configuration, calculate Wh = (mAh ÷ 1,000) × volts. For example, a rated 10,000 mAh at 3.7 V is about 37 Wh before conversion losses. Treat this as nominal stored energy, not the amount that always reaches your phone.

Why advertised capacity is not delivered capacity

A power bank usually converts internal cell voltage to a higher output voltage. Conversion, cabling, battery protection and the receiving device all consume some energy. A 37 Wh pack will therefore provide less than 37 Wh to an attached phone or tablet. Temperature, aging and load also change real results.

Two power banks both marked 10,000 mAh can perform differently when their nominal voltages, tested output conditions and efficiency differ. Product manuals sometimes specify a separate rated output capacity at 5 V; do not confuse this with internal cell mAh.

Compare like with like

For a travel capacity check, Wh is the practical headline number. For a phone battery, the exact model and vendor-reported battery health matter more than a generic conversion. For a USB charger, power in watts says how quickly energy may be delivered, not how much the charger stores.

If voltage is missing, do not silently assume 3.7 V for an unfamiliar pack. Find the model label or official specification first. Our calculator lets you enter a stated voltage rather than hiding the assumption.

Run a transparent estimate

Open the Watt-hour Lab and enter the numbers printed on the pack. Then choose an estimated conversion efficiency and device load to see an illustrative runtime. The output is a planning estimate, not a battery-health reading or a guarantee of real-world endurance.

Keep a screenshot or note of the source label when comparing products. This prevents switching between cell capacity and output capacity halfway through the calculation.

Questions readers ask

Can two 10,000 mAh power banks store different energy?

Yes. Milliamp-hours express charge and must be paired with nominal voltage to calculate energy. Compare labeled Wh values where possible. A capacity printed at internal cell voltage also differs from a quoted output capacity at a regulated USB voltage, so verify what each number describes.

Does a 100 W charger hold 100 Wh?

No. Watts measure a rate of energy transfer, while watt-hours measure stored or transferred energy across time. A wall charger usually stores no meaningful energy. A battery labeled 100 Wh could ideally power a constant 100 W load for one hour before accounting for losses.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A 10,000 mAh pack at 3.7 V corresponds to about 37 Wh. With a hypothetical 80% output efficiency, about 29.6 Wh would be available for an idealized load calculation; actual delivered energy can differ.

Practical checklist

  • Record rated mAh and nominal V from the same battery label.
  • Convert to Wh before comparing different voltages.
  • Keep conversion loss separate from nominal capacity.
  • Avoid equating charger watts with battery watt-hours.
